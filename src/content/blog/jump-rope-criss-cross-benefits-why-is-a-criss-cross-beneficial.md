**Short answer:** A criss cross is a jump rope skill where you cross your arms in front of your body while the rope passes overhead, jump through the smaller loop, then uncross before the next rotation. It's an **arm-path skill, not a harder workout** — the metabolic cost at the same cadence is close to a basic bounce. What it trains is coordination, timing, and shoulder endurance.

That distinction is the whole article. Most content sells the criss cross as an intensity upgrade. It isn't. It's a coordination upgrade that happens to look impressive, and knowing which one you're buying changes how you should practice it.

## What the Criss Cross Actually Is

The rope keeps turning in the same circle it always does. Your arms cross in front of your torso — forearms or elbows stacked — which shrinks the loop the rope has to pass through. You jump through that smaller loop, then pull your arms apart again before the next rotation.

That's it. The feet don't change much. The jump height doesn't change. The rope doesn't change. The only variable is where your hands are.

Most coaches use "criss cross" and "crossover" interchangeably, and for practical purposes they are the same skill. The name that matters is less important than the mechanic: **arms crossed before the rope reaches your feet, arms apart before the next pass.**

The skill was added to the CrossFit Games in 2022, which tells you something about how it's positioned — it's scored as a skill test, not a conditioning test. That's the correct framing. Treat it as a skill to be learned, not a workout to be endured.

## How to Do It: The Progression That Actually Works

The mistake is trying the full movement with the rope on day one. Learn the arm path first, in this order.

### Step 1 — Find the arm position (no rope)

Put the rope in front of you and cross your arms. Look at the size of the hole.

- **Crossed at the wrists:** the hole is too small to jump through. This is the most common beginner error.
- **Crossed at the elbows:** much larger opening, much easier to pass through.

Start at the elbows and touch them together. Once the pattern is automatic, most people shift to crossing at the forearms — more comfortable and slightly faster, but trickier to learn initially.

### Step 2 — Set the starting position

Rope behind you, hands apart, arms relaxed by your sides. Then pull them forward into the cross. Don't start with your hands up near your ears — the rope will hit you in the back of the head.

### Step 3 — Swing through without jumping

This is the step everyone skips and it's the one that teaches timing.

Swing the rope over your head by bringing your arms down, then up and over. **Cross your arms before the rope hits the floor**, then try to catch the rope under your feet by leaning back onto your heels.

You're not jumping. You're teaching your hands when to cross relative to where the rope is.

### Step 4 — Add one jump, but don't shadow cross

Now take a single jump through the crossed loop.

The error to avoid here has a name: the **shadow cross**. That's when you cross your arms, then pull them apart _before_ you actually pass through the loop. It looks like a criss cross from a distance and does nothing.

The arms stay crossed until your feet are through.

### Step 5 — Keep turning with your wrists while crossed

Once you're through the first cross, don't immediately uncross. Stay in the crossed position and keep the rope turning with your wrists for a rotation or two.

This drill does two things: it teaches wrist control in an unfamiliar arm position, and it makes the shadow cross physically impossible.

Your wrists are the power source for everything in jump rope. If your arms are doing big circles instead of your wrists doing small ones, the criss cross will not work — and neither will anything else.

### Step 6 — Come out, then chain them

After you jump through the cross and the rope is in front of your face, bring your arms apart and continue jumping normally.

Then practice entering and exiting without stopping. **Switch which arm is on top after every cross** — being able to cross both ways is the difference between knowing the trick and owning it.

## The Common Errors

| Error                      | What it looks like                                 | The fix                                                      |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Crossing too late**      | Rope hits your feet before your arms are crossed   | Cross while the rope is still overhead, not on the way down  |
| **Shadow cross**           | Arms cross then uncross before you pass through    | Stay crossed until your feet are on the floor                |
| **Crossing at the wrists** | Hole is too small; rope clips your feet every time | Cross at the elbows; the loop gets much bigger               |
| **Big arm circles**        | Shoulders fatigue fast; rope wobbles               | Turn from the wrists, elbows tucked                          |
| **Jumping too high**       | Loud landings; timing gets harder                  | Hop 1–2 inches; you're clearing a thin rope, not a hurdle    |
| **Kicking feet back**      | Heels flick backward and catch the rope            | Shorten the kick; practice running in place without the rope |
| **Rope too long**          | Sluggish loop, catches constantly                  | Size it properly — a loose rope catches feet far more often  |

The bottom three are not criss cross problems. They're basic-bounce problems that the criss cross exposes. If you can't do fifty clean basic bounces with quiet landings, fix that first — the [shin splints guide](/blog/avoid-shin-splints-jump-rope-how-to-avoid-shin-splints/) covers what's going wrong and how to correct it. The criss cross will still be there in two weeks.

## What Muscles It Actually Works

Honest answer: **the same ones as a basic bounce, plus some shoulder and forearm work.** The criss cross is not a different muscle-building stimulus.

| Muscle group                       | Role                                        | Compared to basic bounce |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| **Calves (gastrocnemius, soleus)** | Primary — every landing                     | Same                     |
| **Quads and hamstrings**           | Assist the jump                             | Same                     |
| **Forearms and grip**              | Turn the rope; hold the crossed position    | **More**                 |
| **Shoulders (deltoids)**           | Maintain the crossed arm position           | **More**                 |
| **Upper abs and obliques**         | Stabilize against the changing arm position | Slightly more            |
| **Adductors**                      | Engage if you also cross your feet          | Only if feet cross       |

Sources describing the criss cross list the calves as primary with quads and hamstrings assisting, forearms and upper abs as minor contributors. Fitness sites that promise "shoulders, chest, biceps, triceps" from a criss cross are overselling a movement where the arms are holding a static position, not pressing or pulling anything heavy.

The genuine addition is **shoulder and forearm endurance** — holding your arms in a crossed position while the rope keeps turning is a sustained isometric-ish demand the basic bounce doesn't have. That's real, but it's conditioning, not hypertrophy.

If you also cross your _feet_ (a different skill, sometimes folded into criss cross variations), the adductors and hip flexors join in — that's the leg-crossing component, not the arm component.

## What It Actually Trains

Four benefits, ranked by how real they are.

**1. Coordination and hand-eye timing — the main event.** The criss cross is a dual-task problem: your hands have to do something novel while your feet keep a rhythm that hasn't changed. That's a genuine coordination challenge, and it's why the skill is used as a progression marker in rope sport. Learning it makes other arm variations easier because you've built the "change the arms without changing the feet" pattern.

**2. Shoulder and forearm endurance.** Covered above. Modest but real.

**3. Body awareness under load.** Crossing your arms shifts your center of mass slightly and changes your visual reference. Holding the rhythm through that is a small balance challenge — not a balance exercise, but a balance _demand_.

**4. Variety that keeps you training.** This is underrated and it's the honest one. The criss cross is fun in a way that a basic bounce stops being after week three. Adherence beats optimization.

## The Thing Nobody Tells You: It's Not a Harder Workout

Here's the part that separates this post from the rest of the SERP.

At the same cadence, **a criss cross costs roughly the same energy as a basic bounce** — the legs do identical work and the rope travels an identical path. Published MET values for rope skipping don't distinguish between arm positions; they distinguish between _speeds_. General rope skipping sits around 11 METs, moderate jumping 11.8, and fast jumping 12.3.

So if you're doing 120 skips per minute, you're at roughly 120 skips per minute of work whether your arms are crossed or not. The criss cross is harder to _perform_, not harder to _metabolize_.

For the calorie math, our [per-bodyweight breakdown](/blog/jump-rope-calories-burned-per-hour-how-many-calories/) has the full tables. The short version: cadence and bodyweight drive the number, skill selection barely moves it.

This matters practically. If your session plan says "criss crosses for conditioning," you're not getting more conditioning — you're getting less, because you'll trip more and spend more time resetting. **Use the criss cross to build skill, and use clean continuous bouncing or intervals to build conditioning.** Trying to do both with one movement means doing neither well.

## Criss Cross vs Boxer Skip vs Basic Bounce

|                                    | Basic bounce         | Boxer skip                 | Criss cross                        |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------- | -------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| **What changes**                   | —                    | Footwork (weight shift)    | Arm path                           |
| **Impact per leg**                 | Higher               | Roughly half               | Same as basic                      |
| **Metabolic cost at same cadence** | Baseline             | Same                       | About the same                     |
| **Coordination demand**            | Low                  | Moderate                   | High                               |
| **Learning curve**                 | Minutes              | 1–3 weeks                  | 2–4 weeks                          |
| **Best for**                       | Short hard intervals | Volume, joint preservation | Skill, variety, shoulder endurance |
| **Prerequisite**                   | None                 | Basic bounce               | 50+ clean basic bounces            |

If you're choosing what to learn next and you haven't got the [boxer skip](/blog/jump-rope-boxer-skip-what-is-it-and-how-to-do-it/) yet, do that first. It changes how much volume your legs can tolerate, which matters more for your training than an arm trick does. The criss cross changes how your upper body works. Different problems, different value.

## A Note From Experience

Four years of pro basketball, and the rope was never a skill-development tool for me — it was a warm-up and a finisher. Nobody was teaching criss crosses in a pro gym. The rope work we did was basic bounces, high knees, and running steps, because the goal was to raise heart rate and wake up the feet before practice.

That's not a knock on the criss cross. It's a calibration. The skill is genuinely worth learning if you're building a jump rope practice — but it's a jump-rope-specific skill with limited transfer to other sports. A basketball player gets more from the boxer skip and running step, because those patterns show up in defensive footwork. A boxer gets more from the boxer skip for the same reason. The criss cross is for people who want to get good at jump rope.

Which is a completely legitimate goal. Just know what you're buying.

## Rope Choice for Learning the Criss Cross

The criss cross is more sensitive to rope feedback than the boxer skip, because the arm path is novel and you need to feel where the rope is while your hands are doing something unfamiliar.

**[A weighted rope](/shop/weighted-jump-rope/)** is the better learning tool of the two we build. The heavier cable turns slower and gives you more time to feel the cross. A thin speed cable whips through the loop so fast that beginners often can't tell whether they crossed at the right moment — they just catch their feet and don't know why.

Once the pattern is automatic, a **[speed rope](/shop/speed-jump-rope/)** is the better choice for performance. The faster bearings let you hold a higher cadence through the criss cross without the rope becoming the bottleneck, which is what you want once you're chaining crosses into continuous sets.

For learning, use the weighted rope. For performing, use the speed rope.

## How to Program It

Treat it as a skill block, not a workout block.

**A 10-minute practice session:**

- 2 min basic bounce (warm-up, find rhythm)
- 3 × 10 criss crosses with a reset between each set
- 2 min basic bounce to finish
- Progress to 3 × 20, then 3 × 30, then continuous

**Frequency:** 3–4 short sessions per week beats one long one. Skill acquisition is a frequency game, and ten focused minutes daily will get you there faster than an hour on Saturday.

**Where it fits:** after your warm-up, before your conditioning. Skill work belongs when you're fresh — not at the end of a session when your forearms are cooked and your timing is sloppy. Poorly practiced reps build poorly grooved patterns.

**When to stop:** when you start catching the rope on more than one in five attempts. That's fatigue, not failure. Reset, do some basic bounces, and come back tomorrow. Grinding through bad reps teaches your hands the wrong timing.

If you want to add the criss cross to a conditioning session once the pattern is reliable, the [jump rope ab circuit](/blog/jump-rope-ab-circuit-get-ripped-with-a-jump-rope/) uses it as the final rope interval — the rotation it adds is a genuine core demand that the basic bounce doesn't have.

## Three Fixes for "The Rope Keeps Catching My Feet"

Almost every version of this problem comes down to one of three things.

**1. Timing.** Your arms are crossing on the way down instead of while the rope is overhead. You need to be fully crossed _before_ the rope reaches your feet. Slow the whole thing down and swing without jumping to feel where the cross needs to happen.

**2. The shadow cross.** You cross, then uncross before you pass through. If you're doing this, stay in the crossed position for two full rotations with your wrists turning — you'll feel immediately whether you're actually committed to the cross.

**3. Rope length.** A rope that's too long loops slowly and catches feet far more often than a properly sized one. If you sized your rope for a slow beginner bounce, it may be too long for criss crosses, which need a tighter loop. Standing on the middle of the rope, the handles should reach somewhere between your sternum and your armpit.

If all three are correct and you're still catching, the issue is usually that your basic bounce isn't clean yet. Go back to quiet, low, consistent bouncing for two weeks — the [shin splints guide](/blog/avoid-shin-splints-jump-rope-how-to-avoid-shin-splints/) covers the underlying landing issues if the problem is impact-related. The criss cross gets easier when the foundation underneath it is solid.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a criss cross in jump rope?**
A criss cross (also called a crossover) is a jump rope skill where you cross your arms in front of your body while the rope passes overhead, jump through the smaller loop, then uncross your arms before the next rotation. The rope keeps turning the whole time — it's the arm path that changes, not the jump.

**Do criss crosses burn more calories than regular jumping?**
Barely. The metabolic cost of a criss cross is close to a basic bounce at the same cadence, because your legs do the same work and the rope travels the same distance. What changes is coordination demand, not energy expenditure. If your goal is calorie burn, cadence matters far more than which skill you're doing.

**What muscles does the criss cross work?**
The calves remain the primary driver, with the quads and hamstrings assisting and the forearms and upper abs contributing. The shoulders and deltoids do more work than in a basic bounce because they hold the crossed position, and the adductors engage if you also cross your feet.

**Why does the rope keep catching my feet on the criss cross?**
Three causes, in order of frequency: you're crossing your arms after the rope has already reached your feet instead of before, you're pulling your arms apart too early (the shadow cross), or your rope is too long. Fix the timing first — the arms should be fully crossed while the rope is still overhead.

**Should I cross at the elbows or the forearms?**
Learn at the elbows. Crossing at the wrists leaves a hole too small to jump through; crossing at the elbows creates a much bigger loop. Once the pattern is automatic, most people shift to crossing at the forearms, which feels more natural and is slightly faster.

**How long does it take to learn the criss cross?**
The arm pattern without the rope takes most people one or two sessions. Adding the rope and getting it continuous usually takes two to four weeks of short practice — longer than the boxer skip, because the timing window is tighter. The mistake is trying it at full speed before the arm path is automatic.

**Is the criss cross the same as a boxer cross?**
No, though the names get used loosely. A criss cross is the standard arms-crossed skill. A boxer cross is a variation where you add a side swing or a crossed position into a boxer-skip rhythm. Different footwork underneath, different arm entry.
